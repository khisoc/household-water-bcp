## Overview

This project applies standard BCP and risk-management methods to a real-world scenario: a prolonged water shortage during Jamaica's 2026 dry season. It estimates demand, measures storage resilience, scores risks, and sets out a preventive, response and recovery plan.

**Skills demonstrated:** risk identification and scoring (likelihood × impact), business continuity planning, capacity and resilience analysis, assumption documentation, policy recommendations, and sourcing from official public data.

## 1. Introduction

Water is essential for drinking, cooking, bathing, sanitation, cleaning and laundry, so disruptions to supply create significant continuity risks for households. Jamaica has been experiencing water-supply challenges linked to prolonged dry conditions and below-normal rainfall. The National Water Commission (NWC) reports that drought is affecting a significant number of its systems. As of October 6, 2026, the Mona Reservoir was at approximately 33% of capacity and the Hermitage Dam at approximately 40.5%, both important sources for the Kingston and St. Andrew system (NWC, 2026).

The NWC also reported that 107 of its water systems were affected by drought as of October 1, 2026, noting that some communities may have difficulty receiving piped water and encouraging customers to maintain adequate storage and manage it carefully (Jamaica Information Service [JIS], 2026).

Business Continuity Planning (BCP) identifies potential disruptions and develops strategies that let critical activities continue during and after them. Although usually associated with organizations, the same principles apply to households.

**Purpose:** evaluate a household's preparedness for a prolonged water shortage, estimate consumption, assess storage capacity, identify major risks, and develop a practical Household Water Continuity Plan.

## 2. Household Profile

Bathing (350 L), toilet flushing (280 L) and laundry (175 L) are the largest uses, so they offer the most room for conservation. Drinking and cooking are essential and take priority. During an extended disruption the household should reduce water used for bathing, laundry, cleaning and flushing, for example with shorter showers, full laundry loads and buckets instead of running taps.

Actual duration could be shorter if normal usage continues, so conservation should begin immediately and stored water should be reserved for drinking, cooking and sanitation.

## 5. Risk Assessment

Each risk is rated on a 1 to 5 scale for likelihood (1 = very low, 5 = very high) and impact (1 = very low, 5 = very high). **Risk Rating = Likelihood × Impact.**

| Risk | Likelihood (1–5) | Impact (1–5) | Risk Rating |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Complete interruption of NWC supply | 4 | 5 | 20 – High |
| Household water storage becomes depleted | 4 | 5 | 20 – High |
| Increased difficulty maintaining sanitation | 3 | 5 | 15 – High |
| Insufficient water for drinking and cooking | 3 | 5 | 15 – High |
| Water contamination or poor water quality | 3 | 5 | 15 – High |
| Increased household water costs | 4 | 3 | 12 – Medium |
| Conflict or poor coordination over water use | 3 | 3 | 9 – Medium |

**Analysis**

- **Supply interruption and storage depletion** rate highest because the household depends on NWC as its primary source. A long outage combined with exhausted storage would make essential activities difficult.
- **Water quality** is a critical risk. Water from unfamiliar or untreated alternative sources may not be safe for drinking or cooking, so alternatives must be evaluated before use.
- **Sanitation** is a major concern, since a prolonged shortage affects flushing, bathing, cleaning and hygiene, with consequences for health and comfort.

## 6. Business Continuity Plan

### 6.1 Preventive Actions

- Keep the 2,000-gallon tank at an adequate level and avoid routinely letting it run nearly empty.
- Regularly inspect the tank, pipes, valves and connections for leaks, and repair them promptly.
- Make conservation a normal habit: no running taps, shorter showers, fix leaking faucets and toilets, and run washing machines only with full loads.
- Monitor NWC announcements and advisories (NWC, 2026).

### 6.2 Response Actions

If the NWC supply is interrupted:

1. Determine whether the interruption is temporary or likely to last several days.
2. Immediately reduce non-essential use, prioritizing drinking, cooking, basic hygiene and sanitation.
3. Postpone laundry, vehicle washing and excessive outdoor cleaning. Use a controlled amount of water for bathing.
4. Set a daily water-use limit well below the normal 1,050 L/day.
5. If the outage continues, investigate approved alternatives such as authorized water trucks. Use only sources considered safe for potable use for drinking and cooking.
6. Keep a simple daily record of tank level and consumption so the remaining days of supply are always known.

### 6.3 Recovery Actions

- Refill the tank as soon as supply returns while continuing to conserve.
- Review how much water was used during the disruption and which activities drove consumption, then use this to improve the plan.
- Repair any damage or leaks found, and inspect the tank to confirm it is clean and suitable for future use.
- Maintain a minimum emergency reserve rather than fully consuming storage once service resumes.

## 7. Future Preparedness Strategy

If water levels stay below normal for the rest of the dry season, conservation and storage management should be treated as ongoing preparedness.

- **Conservation:** shorter showers, taps off when not in use, leak repairs, water-efficient cleaning, full laundry loads, and safe reuse of suitable water for non-potable purposes where it creates no health or sanitation risk.
- **Additional storage:** seven adults create relatively high demand. Properly maintained drums or a second approved tank would increase resilience if interruptions become longer or more frequent.
- **Alternative sources:** identify reliable options before an emergency, such as authorized water-trucking. Keep potable water for drinking and cooking and use non-potable water only for suitable tasks.
- **Household awareness:** all seven members should know where the tank is, its approximate capacity, which uses to cut first, and that drinking and cooking have priority. Simple rule: *when NWC supply is interrupted, non-essential use is reduced immediately.*
- **Long-term monitoring:** keep tracking NWC reservoir levels and advisories, and periodically review emergency water requirements, increasing storage if drought continues.

## 8. Conclusion

Using BCP principles, this assessment found the household to have a **moderate** level of preparedness. Its main strengths are a substantial 2,000-gallon tank (about 7,570 L when full) and a currently reliable NWC supply. At roughly 1,050 L/day, a full tank supports about seven days of normal use, and conservation focused on essential activities could extend that significantly.

The highest-priority risks are interruption of the main supply and depletion of stored water. Reliance on a single source is the key vulnerability. The most important improvements are:

1. Maintain adequate water reserves.
2. Increase storage capacity where possible.
3. Practise continuous conservation.
4. Monitor NWC advisories.
5. Establish alternative water sources.
6. Make sure every household member understands the plan.

## 9. Policy Recommendations

*Recommendations to the National Water Commission, local authorities and community groups.*

1. **Expand community water storage.** Shared emergency storage could add resilience where individual households lack sufficient capacity.
2. **Improve early-warning communication.** Timely information on reservoir levels, expected restrictions and drought conditions lets households conserve and store water before a major interruption.
3. **Strengthen emergency water distribution.** Clear procedures, authorized water-trucking and designated community distribution points would help ensure affected and vulnerable households can access essential water.
4. **Promote household water conservation.** Public education should present conservation as a routine practice, not only a drought response.
5. **Encourage increased household storage.** A 2,000-gallon tank offers valuable protection, but a prolonged disruption can eventually exceed even that capacity.
